The cryptography that holds every Bitcoin and Ethereum balance, written from first principles in plain JavaScript and run live in your browser. No crypto libraries: the curve, Keccak-256, ECDSA, Schnorr and Shamir are all in lib/.

Each section runs real code on real 256-bit numbers, and the last few break and rebuild private keys.

Experiment 1

Adding points on a finite curve

Over the real numbers, y² = x³ + 7 is a smooth curve. Over a finite field it becomes a scatter of points, but the addition rule still works: draw the line through P and Q, find the third point it hits, reflect it.

Click two points. Then multiply: k·P is P added to itself k times, and walking k backwards from the result is the discrete-log problem that keeps your keys safe.

Experiment 2

A keypair and an Ethereum address

A private key is a random number d below the group order n. The public key is the point d·G. The address is the last 20 bytes of the Keccak-256 hash of that point, with the EIP-55 checksum mixing upper and lower case.

Experiment 3

Sign, verify, recover

Ethereum's personal_sign prefixes the message and hashes it with Keccak. ECDSA then picks a nonce k (here derived deterministically per RFC 6979), sets r to the x-coordinate of k·G, and solves s = k⁻¹(z + r·d).

The extra byte v lets anyone recover the signer's public key from the signature alone. That is how a contract or a login page knows who signed.

Experiment 4

Reuse a nonce, lose the key

If the same k signs two different messages, both signatures share the same r, and two equations in two unknowns give up the private key with grade-school algebra.

This is how Sony's PS3 signing key was extracted in 2010, and how a 2013 Android RNG bug emptied Bitcoin wallets. It is the reason RFC 6979 exists.

Victim signs “send 1 ETH to Alice” and “send 1 ETH to Bob” with the same k.
An observer notices r₁ = r₂. Same r means same k·G, so the same k.
Subtract the two signing equations. s₁ − s₂ = k⁻¹(z₁ − z₂), so k = (z₁ − z₂) / (s₁ − s₂) mod n.
Solve for d. d = (s₁·k − z₁) / r mod n.
Experiment 5

Schnorr, the Taproot signature

BIP-340 Schnorr signs with s = k + e·d, where e hashes the nonce point, the key and the message. It is linear, so keys and signatures from several parties can be added into one. That is what makes MuSig multisigs look like single-key spends on Bitcoin.

Public keys shrink to 32 bytes by fixing y to be even.

Experiment 6

Split the key three of five

Shamir's scheme hides d as the constant term of a random degree-2 polynomial and hands out five points on it. Any three points pin down the polynomial; two reveal nothing at all.

Pick shares and rebuild. Lagrange interpolation at x = 0 does the work.

Under the hood

lib/ec.js field and curve arithmetic, double-and-add. lib/keccak.js Keccak-f[1600] from the spec. lib/sigs.js RFC 6979, ECDSA, recovery, the nonce-reuse solver, BIP-340. lib/shamir.js polynomial split and Lagrange. SHA-256 and HMAC come from WebCrypto.

Verified

Public keys and signatures are cross-checked against OpenSSL (Node's crypto) in both directions, plus Keccak, EIP-55 and BIP-340 published vectors. Ten tests, npm test.
